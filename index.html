Sources, assumptions and limitations

The point

Build something you can argue with before the real schedule exists. This study uses AI to help turn two sample schedules into one 4D view of steel erection and concrete placement, so you can question the sequence, crew handoffs and conflicts.

Where it comes from

The grid and framing are based on public 2019 bid drawings for the OSU College of Osteopathic Medicine at the Cherokee Nation in Tahlequah, Oklahoma. Structural drawings: Chavez-Grieves. Architect: James R. Childers Architect.

What is assumed

The schedules and conflicts are hypothetical. Bays are simplified; member sizes, piece weights and concrete quantities are illustrative. Crew allocations, cure times and crane capacities are modeled assumptions. The Data & rules tab explains how the handoffs are found; Picks lists the picks and crane-chart references.

For learning and discussion. This is not a lift plan or a project schedule. Crane capacities are interpolated from published reference charts for demonstration only. Real lifts require the manufacturer’s charts for the actual configuration and a qualified lift planner.

Baseline for the Ghost view baseline: original dates

Ghost compares what is in place today against what the baseline said should be in place. Red is behind, green is ahead. With no baseline loaded it compares against the original dates, so any sequence you slip shows up immediately.

Rules

  1. Concrete to steel. Every steel raise that starts on foundations needs every foundation pour whose footprint overlaps it. Available = pour finish + cure days (the OSHA 1926.752 75% letter). Need = raise start.
  2. Steel to concrete. Every elevated pour needs every steel release at the same level whose footprint overlaps it. Available = milestone date. Need = first activity of the pour (pour stops / rebar).
  3. Stacked trade note. A pour placed while ironworkers are still decking a higher level in the same footprint gets flagged, not failed.
  4. Crews. One ironworker gang of 8: on a raise day 6 on the iron and 2 hooking on in the yard, the rest to decking, then bolt-up, then weld-out. Concrete crews by activity: 8 carpenters forming, 8 rodbusters on deck prep, 10 finishers on pour day, 2 on cure and strip. Two trades in the same footprint on the same or adjacent level turn red.
  5. Pump and crane. The pump sets up on the north side at the pour's center and reaches to it (170' class boom). A pour day that overlaps a crane raise in the same bays gets flagged: shared airspace, hose and boom under the swing.
  6. Missing tie. If the needed activity (or anything in its group) is not upstream of the needer's predecessors, the handoff is marked. Dates can look fine today and still break the first time a pour slips.

Sample set

Grid A to J by 1 to 4, about 260' x 94'. Sector 1 (A to E) on spread footings with slab on grade (S1.11); Sector 2 (E to J) has a basement at 84'-0" with concrete walls and pilasters (S1.02, S2.03). Levels: L1 100'-0", L2 118'-0", L3 134'-0", roof deck bearing 150' to 152'. Columns W24x162/192 in the lower tier with W24x104 above (S2.01/S2.02), so erection runs in two tiers: basement lid first, tier 1 to L2, tier 2 to roof. SidePlate perimeter moment frames (W36x160 at L2, W24x76 at L3, W24x68 at roof), W18x35 (30) infill at 30' bays, W33x291 (110) girders cambered 3/4", unshored composite deck type D6R. Elevated pours break at D and G, so each middle pour straddles two erection zones. Numbers in parentheses on members are shear stud counts.
